# Digital Signal Processing 101

**Category:** Engineering
**Subcategory:** Digital Signal Processing
**Tags:** DSP, digital signal processing, signals, sampling, Nyquist, Fourier transform, DFT, FFT, filters, convolution
**Type:** concept

## 1. Short Answer

**Digital Signal Processing (DSP)** is the study of representing, analyzing, and manipulating signals using digital computers or digital hardware.

A signal might be:

* audio
* music
* speech
* sensor measurements
* radio signals
* images
* biomedical measurements
* vibration
* radar/sonar data

The fundamental DSP pipeline is:

```text
Physical world
     │
     ▼
 Analog signal
     │
     │ ADC
     ▼
Discrete-time signal
     │
     ▼
 DSP algorithms
     │
     ├── filtering
     ├── Fourier analysis
     ├── compression
     ├── detection
     ├── modulation
     └── feature extraction
     │
     ▼
Processed digital signal
     │
     │ DAC
     ▼
 Analog signal
     │
     ▼
 Physical world
```

The four concepts that form the foundation of DSP are:

```text
Sampling
   +
Quantization
   +
Filtering
   +
Frequency analysis
```

If you understand these, most introductory DSP starts to make sense.

---

# 2. What Is a Signal?

A **signal** is something that carries information and varies according to some independent variable, usually time.

For example, an audio waveform can be represented as:

$$
x(t)
$$

where:

* \(t\) = time
* \(x(t)\) = signal amplitude at time \(t\)

A microphone converts variations in air pressure into an electrical signal.

Conceptually:

```text
Air pressure
     │
     ▼
 Microphone
     │
     ▼
Voltage waveform
     │
     ▼
x(t)
```

A simple sine wave is:

$$
x(t)=A\sin(2\pi ft+\phi)
$$

where:

| Symbol   | Meaning   |
| -------- | --------- |
| \(A\)    | amplitude |
| \(f\)    | frequency |
| \(t\)    | time      |
| \(\phi\) | phase     |

For example:

$$
x(t)=\sin(2\pi 440t)
$$

is a 440 Hz sine wave.

---

# 3. Analog vs Digital Signals

An **analog signal** is continuous in time.

```text
x(t)

 ^
 |       /\
 |      /  \
 |     /    \
 |____/      \____
 |
 +-----------------> t
```

A digital system generally works with a sequence of discrete samples:

```text
x[n]

 ^
 |       ●
 |      ●
 |    ●
 |  ●       ●
 | ●         ●
 +-----------------> n
```

Instead of:

$$
x(t)
$$

we work with:

$$
x[n]
$$

The distinction is important:

* \(x(t)\) → continuous-time signal
* \(x[n]\) → discrete-time signal

---

# 4. Sampling

Sampling converts a continuous-time signal into a sequence of samples.

Suppose:

```text
Analog waveform
       │
       │ sample every T seconds
       ▼
●   ●   ●   ●   ●   ●
```

Mathematically:

$$
x[n]=x(nT_s)
$$

where:

* \(T_s\) = sampling period
* \(f_s\) = sampling frequency

and:

$$
f_s=\frac{1}{T_s}
$$

For example, CD audio uses:

$$
f_s=44,100\text{ Hz}
$$

meaning:

> 44,100 samples are taken every second.

---

# 5. Why Sampling Works

The crucial result is the **Nyquist-Shannon sampling theorem**.

If a signal contains no frequencies above \(f_{max}\), then theoretically it can be perfectly reconstructed if:

$$
f_s > 2f_{max}
$$

So if the highest frequency is:

$$
20\,000\text{ Hz}
$$

you need a sampling frequency greater than:

$$
40\,000\text{ Hz}
$$

This is why audio systems commonly use:

```text
44.1 kHz
48 kHz
96 kHz
192 kHz
```

The sampling rate must be high enough to represent the desired bandwidth.

---

# 6. Aliasing

Sampling below the required rate produces **aliasing**.

Suppose the sampling rate is:

$$
f_s=10\,000\text{ Hz}
$$

The Nyquist frequency is:

$$
f_N=\frac{f_s}{2}=5\,000\text{ Hz}
$$

Now imagine the input contains:

$$
7\,000\text{ Hz}
$$

That frequency cannot be represented correctly.

It can appear as a lower frequency:

$$
f_{alias}=|f-f_s|
$$

so:

$$
|7000-10000|=3000\text{ Hz}
$$

The digital system may therefore see a **3 kHz signal instead of 7 kHz**.

This is aliasing.

```text
Original
7 kHz
   │
   │ insufficient sampling
   ▼
Digital system
   │
   ▼
appears as 3 kHz
```

And importantly:

> **Aliasing cannot be fixed after sampling.**

Once different analog frequencies become indistinguishable in the sampled representation, the lost information cannot be recovered.

---

# 7. Anti-Aliasing Filter

Because of aliasing, an ADC system normally includes a low-pass filter before sampling.

```text
Analog input
     │
     ▼
Anti-aliasing
low-pass filter
     │
     ▼
    ADC
     │
     ▼
Digital signal
```

The filter removes frequencies above the usable bandwidth before the ADC sees them.

For example:

```text
        Passband        Stopband
-----------|------------------------>
           fs/2

<--- allowed --->|<--- remove ------>
```

This is one of the most important practical applications of DSP theory.

---

# 8. Quantization

Sampling discretizes **time**.

Quantization discretizes **amplitude**.

Imagine an ADC with 3 bits.

It has:

$$
2^3=8
$$

possible amplitude levels.

```text
Amplitude

 7 ─────────────
 6 ─────────────
 5 ─────────────
 4 ─────────────
 3 ─────────────
 2 ─────────────
 1 ─────────────
 0 ─────────────
```

The actual analog amplitude must be rounded to one of those levels.

This introduces **quantization error**.

$$
e[n]=x[n]-x_q[n]
$$

where:

* \(x[n]\) = ideal sample
* \(x_q[n]\) = quantized sample

---

# 9. Bit Depth

For an \(N\)-bit ADC:

$$
L=2^N
$$

quantization levels are available.

Examples:

| Bits |     Levels |
| ---: | ---------: |
|    8 |        256 |
|   12 |       4096 |
|   16 |     65,536 |
|   24 | 16,777,216 |

Increasing bit depth gives finer amplitude resolution.

This generally reduces quantization noise.

---

# 10. The Complete ADC Process

Putting the pieces together:

```text
             Analog world
                  │
                  ▼
             Analog signal
                  │
                  ▼
        Anti-aliasing filter
                  │
                  ▼
                 ADC
             ┌────┴────┐
             │         │
         Sampling   Quantization
             │         │
             └────┬────┘
                  │
                  ▼
           Digital samples
```

The result is something like:

```text
x[0], x[1], x[2], x[3], ...
```

DSP algorithms operate on this sequence.

---

# 11. Time Domain

One way to look at a signal is in the **time domain**.

For example:

```text
Amplitude
   ^
   |      /\       /\
   |     /  \     /  \
   |____/____\___/____\____
   |
   +------------------------> time
```

You see:

* amplitude
* timing
* transients
* waveform shape

This is useful for understanding what the signal does over time.

But sometimes you want to know:

> "What frequencies are present?"

For that we use the frequency domain.

---

# 12. Frequency Domain

A Fourier transform decomposes a signal into sinusoidal components.

Instead of:

```text
amplitude vs time
```

we get something like:

```text
amplitude
   ^
   |       |
   |       |
   |       |          |
   |_______|__________|____> frequency
          440       880
```

For example, a musical note might contain:

```text
440 Hz
880 Hz
1320 Hz
1760 Hz
...
```

These components are called **harmonics** when they occur at integer multiples of a fundamental frequency.

---

# 13. Fourier Transform

The Fourier transform is one of the central mathematical tools in DSP.

Conceptually:

$$
\text{time domain}
\quad\xrightarrow{\text{Fourier Transform}}\quad
\text{frequency domain}
$$

and:

$$
\text{frequency domain}
\quad\xrightarrow{\text{Inverse Fourier Transform}}\quad
\text{time domain}
$$

For a continuous signal:

$$
X(f)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}x(t)e^{-j2\pi ft}\,dt
$$

Don't worry about memorizing the equation initially.

The important idea is:

> **The Fourier transform tells you how much of each frequency exists in a signal.**

---

# 14. DFT

Real digital systems work with finite sequences.

For:

$$
x[0],x[1],...,x[N-1]
$$

we commonly use the **Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT)**.

$$
X[k]=
\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}
x[n]e^{-j2\pi kn/N}
$$

The DFT produces:

```text
N time-domain samples
        │
        ▼
       DFT
        │
        ▼
N frequency-domain values
```

Each \(X[k]\) corresponds to a frequency bin.

---

# 15. FFT

The **Fast Fourier Transform (FFT)** is an efficient algorithm for computing the DFT.

This distinction is important:

```text
DFT = mathematical transform

FFT = efficient algorithm for computing the DFT
```

The naive DFT requires approximately:

$$
O(N^2)
$$

operations.

A typical FFT algorithm requires approximately:

$$
O(N\log N)
$$

This difference is enormous for large \(N\).

For example:

```text
N = 1,048,576

DFT:
~N² operations

FFT:
~N log₂N operations
≈ 20 million
```

This is why FFTs are everywhere in DSP.

---

# 16. Frequency Resolution

Suppose you collect:

$$
N
$$

samples at sampling rate:

$$
f_s
$$

The DFT bin spacing is:

$$
\Delta f=\frac{f_s}{N}
$$

For example:

```text
fs = 48,000 Hz
N  = 4,800 samples
```

Then:

$$
\Delta f=10\text{ Hz}
$$

So the frequency bins are:

```text
0 Hz
10 Hz
20 Hz
30 Hz
...
```

To get finer frequency resolution, you generally need a longer observation interval.

---

# 17. Filters

A **filter** modifies the frequency content of a signal.

The four basic types are:

```text
Low-pass
High-pass
Band-pass
Band-stop
```

---

## Low-pass

Allows low frequencies through:

```text
Gain
 ^
 |████████████
 |            \
 |             \
 |              \____
 +--------------------> frequency
```

Applications:

* remove high-frequency noise
* smoothing
* anti-aliasing
* audio tone shaping

---

## High-pass

Allows high frequencies through:

```text
Gain
 ^
 |          _________
 |         /
 |        /
 |_______/
 +--------------------> frequency
```

Applications:

* remove DC
* remove slow drift
* edge detection
* remove low-frequency noise

---

## Band-pass

Allows a range of frequencies:

```text
Gain
 ^
 |        ______
 |       /      \
 |______/        \____
 +--------------------> frequency
```

Applications:

* radio receivers
* speech processing
* instrument extraction

---

## Band-stop

Rejects a frequency range:

```text
Gain
 ^
 |───────\      /──────
 |        \____/
 |
 +--------------------> frequency
```

A narrow band-stop filter is often called a **notch filter**.

---

# 18. FIR Filters

**FIR** means:

> Finite Impulse Response

An FIR filter can be expressed as:

$$
y[n]=\sum_{k=0}^{M}b_kx[n-k]
$$

For example:

$$
y[n]=
0.25x[n]
+0.5x[n-1]
+0.25x[n-2]
$$

This is a simple smoothing filter.

Conceptually:

```text
x[n] ──┬─────────────┐
       │             │
      delay         × coefficient
       │
      delay
       │
       └─────────────┐
                     ▼
                   SUM
                     │
                     ▼
                   y[n]
```

FIR filters are often attractive because they can have:

* exact finite impulse response
* straightforward implementation
* linear-phase designs
* guaranteed stability for ordinary finite coefficients

---

# 19. IIR Filters

**IIR** means:

> Infinite Impulse Response

An IIR filter uses feedback.

A generic form is:

$$
y[n]=
\sum_{k=0}^{M}b_kx[n-k]
-
\sum_{k=1}^{N}a_ky[n-k]
$$

Conceptually:

```text
             ┌───────────────┐
             │               │
x[n] ───────>│    Filter     ├──────> y[n]
             │               │
             └───────▲───────┘
                     │
                     └── feedback
```

IIR filters can achieve sharp frequency responses with fewer coefficients than many FIR designs.

Common examples include:

* Butterworth
* Chebyshev
* elliptic
* Bessel

---

# 20. FIR vs IIR

|                  | FIR                                  | IIR                 |
| ---------------- | ------------------------------------ | ------------------- |
| Feedback         | No                                   | Yes                 |
| Impulse response | Finite                               | Infinite            |
| Stability        | Easier to guarantee                  | Must be considered  |
| Linear phase     | Easy                                 | More difficult      |
| Computation      | Often more coefficients              | Often fewer         |
| Memory           | Can be larger                        | Often smaller       |
| Common use       | Audio, communications, precise phase | Efficient filtering |

Neither is universally better.

---

# 21. Convolution

Convolution is one of the fundamental operations in DSP.

For discrete signals:

$$
y[n]=x[n]*h[n]
$$

where:

* \(x[n]\) = input
* \(h[n]\) = impulse response
* \(y[n]\) = output

Mathematically:

$$
y[n]=\sum_kx[k]h[n-k]
$$

The critical idea is:

> **An LTI system can be completely characterized by its impulse response.**

And:

```text
input * impulse response
             =
          output
```

This gives:

```text
x[n] ──┐
       │
       ▼
   convolution
       ▲
       │
      h[n]
       │
       ▼
      y[n]
```

---

# 22. Convolution and Filtering

A filter can be viewed as convolution with its impulse response.

For an FIR filter:

```text
Input
  │
  ▼
x[n]
  │
  ├──× b0 ──┐
  ├──delay──× b1 ──┤
  ├──delay──× b2 ──┤
  │                │
  └────────────────▼
                   Σ
                   │
                   ▼
                  y[n]
```

This is essentially a convolution engine.

---

# 23. The Convolution Theorem

One of the most useful relationships in DSP is:

$$
x[n]*h[n]
$$

in the time domain corresponds to:

$$
X(f)H(f)
$$

in the frequency domain.

In other words:

```text
Time domain:

x * h
  │
  ▼
 y


Frequency domain:

X × H
  │
  ▼
 Y
```

This is why FFT-based filtering can be extremely efficient for large signals.

---

# 24. Impulse Response

An impulse is:

$$
\delta[n]
$$

defined as:

$$
\delta[0]=1
$$

and:

$$
\delta[n]=0 \quad n\neq0
$$

If you feed an LTI system an impulse:

```text
δ[n]
 │
 ▼
System
 │
 ▼
h[n]
```

the output is its **impulse response**.

The impulse response tells us how the system responds to arbitrary inputs.

---

# 25. LTI Systems

A huge portion of introductory DSP revolves around **Linear Time-Invariant (LTI)** systems.

### Linear

If:

$$
x_1 \rightarrow y_1
$$

and:

$$
x_2 \rightarrow y_2
$$

then:

$$
ax_1+bx_2
$$

produces:

$$
ay_1+by_2
$$

### Time invariant

If delaying the input delays the output by the same amount, the system is time invariant.

For LTI systems:

$$
y[n]=x[n]*h[n]
$$

This makes convolution extraordinarily important.

---

# 26. Z-Transform

The **Z-transform** is another fundamental DSP tool.

It is particularly useful for discrete-time systems and digital filters.

Conceptually:

```text
Time-domain difference equation
             │
             ▼
         Z-transform
             │
             ▼
     algebraic expression
             │
             ▼
       system analysis
```

For example, a difference equation such as:

$$
y[n]=x[n]+ay[n-1]
$$

can be analyzed in the Z-domain.

The transfer function becomes:

$$
H(z)=\frac{Y(z)}{X(z)}
$$

For this example:

$$
H(z)=\frac{1}{1-az^{-1}}
$$

The Z-transform gives us tools for studying:

* stability
* poles
* zeros
* filter behavior
* difference equations

---

# 27. Poles and Zeros

Digital filters are often described using **poles and zeros**.

For example:

$$
H(z)=
\frac{(z-z_1)(z-z_2)}
{(z-p_1)(z-p_2)}
$$

where:

```text
zeros → frequencies/features being attenuated
poles → frequencies/features being emphasized
```

A pole-zero plot might look like:

```text
             Im
              ^
          ×   |       ○
              |
     ---------+----------> Re
              |
          ○   |
              |
```

The exact interpretation depends on the system and location in the complex plane.

For causal LTI systems, pole locations are also central to stability.

---

# 28. Sampling ↔ Frequency Domain

Sampling has an important frequency-domain interpretation.

Sampling a signal causes its spectrum to repeat around multiples of the sampling frequency.

Conceptually:

```text
Spectrum

        replica       replica       replica
           /\            /\            /\
__________/  \__________/  \__________/  \____
        -fs            0             fs
```

If the spectral replicas overlap:

```text
        /\ 
_______/  \____
       \  /
        \/
```

you get aliasing.

This is the frequency-domain explanation of the Nyquist theorem.

# 31. DSP and Real-Time Processing

DSP is often used in real time.

For example:

```text
Audio input
    │
    ▼
[block of samples]
    │
    ▼
DSP
    │
    ▼
[processed block]
    │
    ▼
Audio output
```

Instead of processing one sample at a time, many systems process blocks:

```text
128 samples
256 samples
512 samples
1024 samples
```

This is called **block processing**.

There is a trade-off:

```text
smaller block
    ↓
lower latency
    ↓
more processing overhead


larger block
    ↓
higher latency
    ↓
often more efficient
```

This is extremely important in real-time audio.
